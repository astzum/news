# Fri 18 Sep 2026

**The three that matter:** NVIDIA is shipping two toolchains that compile Rust straight
to PTX, closing the last gap where the kernel itself had to be written in another
language. Z.ai stood up production inference for GLM-5.3-Flash on a cluster of 100,000+
Chinese-made accelerators, and most of the optimisation work was done by an agent running
GLM-5.3. And someone is running fake job interviews at Rust maintainers to get code onto
their machines.

## Dev + infra

### NVIDIA will let you write GPU kernels in Rust, on two tracks, neither production-ready
`cuda-oxide` is a custom `rustc` codegen backend: `#[kernel]` functions go through Rust
MIR, the Pliron IR framework and LLVM down to PTX, and everything else falls through to
the normal backend. `cutile-rs` is the Tile track — you write what one tile of data does
and the Tile IR compiler decides how it maps onto each architecture. The safety argument
is in the type system rather than bolted on: `DisjointSlice` splits one mutable borrow
into per-thread pieces so every thread gets exclusive access to its own element, thread
indices are their own type rather than integers, and `#[launch_contract]` makes the launch
config get validated against the kernel's declared shape and the live device limits before
you get a safe launch method. Requirements differ: `cuda-oxide` needs Linux, compute
capability 8.0+, CUDA 12.x+, clang with libclang headers and the pinned
`nightly-2026-04-03`; `cutile-rs` runs on stable Rust 1.89+ with CUDA 13.3 and no custom
LLVM. NVIDIA's own advice is to reach for Tile first and drop to SIMT when you need the
control. Temper expectations — `cuda-oxide` is early alpha and the post says plainly that
neither is production-ready, though `cutile-rs` is on crates.io and already in Hugging
Face's Grout and mistral.rs. The post went up 8 September and only surfaced widely this
week.
[announcement](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/introducing-cuda-rust-two-tracks-for-writing-gpu-kernels/)

### Someone is booking fake video calls with Rust maintainers to get code onto their machines
The Rust project put out an advisory about an active campaign against rust-lang members
and owners of popular crates. The approach is social, not technical: a plausible company
with a LinkedIn presence makes contact about a job, a contract or a project, the
conversation moves to a video call, and on that call the target is talked into installing
something or running a command. The advisory names no confirmed compromise, which is
worth reading as "we don't know yet" rather than "nothing happened". Practical version if
you own a crate: initiate calls yourself on a platform you already trust, check MFA and
recent logins on your crates.io and GitHub accounts now rather than after, and mail
help@crates.io or security@rust-lang.org if something feels wrong.
[advisory](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2026/09/17/targeted-attacks/)

### GitLab.com rate limits become tier-based on 19 October, and anonymous traffic gets 60/hour
Limits will apply per user and per top-level group according to subscription plan. Free
and unauthenticated traffic moves first on 19 October 2026; Premium and Ultimate follow in
January 2027. The number to check against your automation is the unauthenticated one: 60
requests per hour per IP, applied regardless of origin — including scripts hitting a paid
account without credentials, which is the failure mode most likely to bite. There are two
brownout windows to test against beforehand, 7 and 14 October, 15:00–19:00 UTC both days,
where the new limits are switched on and back off. GitLab says almost all users are
already inside the new limits; the per-plan numbers are in the rate limits docs rather
than the post.
[blog](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/rate-limit-change-2026/)

## AI industry

### Mozilla puts the open-weight capability gap at four months and 60% of the price
Version 1.1 of the State of Open Source AI report, data current to 1 September. The
headline finding: the best open model trails the closed leader by three points at 60% of
the price, and on long-horizon tasks open trails closed by roughly four months — with the
useful caveat that "under eight hours either model does the job; past twelve, neither
does," so the gap only exists in a narrow band. The frontier's top four are closed and the
next four are open. Eight of the top ten models by token volume on OpenRouter in August
were open weights, yet 96% of 2025 model-layer revenue went to closed providers. The
survey underneath is Mozilla/SlashData, n=1,411 professional developers, 79% of whom use
open models against 71% closed. Note what the four-month figure is: a fit to METR
task-horizon data, not a benchmark score, so treat it as a trend line rather than a
measurement.
[report](https://stateofopensource.ai/)

### Anthropic says Claude "leads" 26% of its own AI R&D, and publishes the monitoring numbers
Three proposed metrics, each with an internal snapshot attached. On automation, using
Epoch AI's AL0–AL5 scale: as of August 2026 Claude leads 26% of Anthropic's AI R&D work,
work at or above "AI collaborates" is over 90%, and nothing measured is fully autonomous.
On oversight: ~30,000 agents doing research and engineering at any one time on the
most-used internal platform, 100% of actions passing an online monitor before execution,
and of over a billion decisions in August, 0.002% — about 1 in 47,000 — blocked. Offline
monitoring flags roughly 100,000 transcripts a week, of which about 50 reach a human. On
compute, for the week of 13–20 July: about 6% of AI R&D compute went to safety, rising to
about 12% of the compute for AI-driven AI R&D. The interesting part isn't the numbers, it's
that they are defined well enough for another lab to publish the same ones — which is the
stated point, alongside a plan to embed third-party evaluators internally.
[post](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/measuring-pace-of-ai-development)

## AI research + releases

### GLM-5.3-Flash runs on 100,000 domestic accelerators, and an agent did most of the tuning
Z.ai's writeup of bringing up production inference from scratch on Chinese-made silicon,
against limited chip memory and bandwidth, incomplete kernel support and a 1M-token
context window. Two weeks from first successful run to production, with roughly 3x
end-to-end throughput over the initial baseline — EPD disaggregation, W8A8, INT8/FP8/BF16
mixed-precision cache quantisation, ReplaySSM, Layer Split. The transferable idea is what
they call "dense feedback": end-to-end metrics like "TTFT up 30%" tell an agent it failed
but not where, so they wired kernel-level correctness comparisons, microbenchmarks,
execution traces and runtime events into the agent's loop so every hypothesis has a cheap
local way to be tested. Two concrete finds make the case better than the throughput chart
does. The agent traced a numerical error to a KDA kernel defaulting to TF32 on FP32 inputs
in the context-parallel path, fixed upstream in Flash Linear Attention. And it tracked a
>20% prefill regression to DeepEP v1.2.1 not releasing the Python GIL across its C++ calls,
which starved the Mooncake Transfer thread — after the fix the gap fell under 1%. Read the
"recursive self-improvement" framing with a raised eyebrow; the engineering underneath is
the real article.
[writeup](https://z.ai/blog/glm-built-its-inference-infrastructure)

## Homelab

### Ternary models have been paying for zeros they don't have — BITCOS gets to 1.485 bits/weight
Ternary LLMs store weights as {-1, 0, +1}, and the standard five-trit-per-byte packing
costs 1.625 bits per weight because it assumes the three symbols are equally likely. They
aren't: across 29 ternary models the authors measured zeros at up to 51.5% of all weights.
BITCOS is a presence bitmap plus a compacted sign vector, costing 2−z bits per weight at
zero density z, which beats five-trit packing in 26 of the 29 models and hits 1.485
bits/weight on the sparsest. What makes it more than a storage trick is that they wrote
the unpacking sequences — AVX-512, AVX2, Intel Xe2 — so it survives contact with real
kernels: up to 1.28x on matrix-vector multiply against production ternary kernels, and
end-to-end decode throughput up to 1.18x on CPUs and 1.27x on GPUs across five platforms
including client CPUs and integrated Xe2. This is squarely aimed at the machine without a
big GPU in it.
[paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16338)

### CrowdSec's private repos leaked in May via a backdoored TanStack component
Disclosed on 17 September after being informed the day before. The private side — SaaS
console source, some AWS routines, connectors, automations — was read out of GitHub back
in May; the 300-repository figure doing the rounds includes the 130+ that are public by
design. CrowdSec's assessment is that the likely vector was the TanStack compromise, the
same one implicated at Mistral: a backdoored component in their org extracted an API key
with read access to the private codebase, exploitable only during a short window in May.
They say no client data, credentials or PII were involved, that they store neither PII nor
client logs, that a hunt for tokens enabling lateral movement found nothing so far, and
that all relevant credentials were rotated. If you run the Security Engine, that's the
public FOSS side and out of scope. The part worth internalising is the four-month gap
between the key being used and anyone noticing.
[statement](https://www.crowdsec.net/blog/crowdsec-statement-source-code-exposure)
