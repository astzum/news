# Sat 19 Sep 2026

**The three that matter:** Four unrelated Linux local-root vulnerabilities came out of
embargo at once, all in networking paths that ordinary distro kernels enable by default.
A US special operations analyst asked a chatbot about a Chinese ship's manifest, wrote the
answer up as an intelligence report, and planes were in the air before anyone checked.
And Zhipu's ZCode desktop app silently packs your entire repository — full git history
included — encrypts it with a key only Zhipu holds, and uploads it to Aliyun OSS.

## Dev + infra

### Four Linux local-root bugs dropped together, and the pre-reqs are not exotic
Asim Manizada posted DirtyAH6 (CVE-2026-80844), TUNderflow (CVE-2026-81000), PPPoEject
(CVE-2026-68121) and DiagSpill (CVE-2026-74469) to oss-sec on 18 September, after a
linux-distros embargo agreed in mid-July expired. The underlying bugs are 10–21 years old.
The first three need either unprivileged user namespaces or CAP_NET_ADMIN-ish capability
over an attacker-controlled netns; DiagSpill needs neither — just SCTP and `sctp_diag`
support. All four have working local-user-to-root PoCs, and Manizada reports AppArmor and
SELinux did not block them in testing, except where Ubuntu blocks unprivileged user
namespaces outright. The first upstream stable releases carrying all four fixes are
5.10.270, 5.15.221, 6.1.188, 6.6.157, 6.12.109, 6.18.50 and 7.2.4 — check yours against
that list, not against a distro version string. Two also have remote angles: DirtyAH6 can
be a remote crash on an IPv6 router doing AH transport mode (and was groomed to remote root
in a lab), and DiagSpill can be a remote DoS if ASCONF/ADD-IP is on, which it isn't by
default. All four can corrupt the host kernel from inside a container, DiagSpill without
any special capabilities; he didn't chase the container escape but says nothing obvious
stops it.
[disclosure](https://seclists.org/oss-sec/2026/q3/822)

### Cloudflare cut 100TB of RAM by proving most of its hash ring was doing nothing
Their Pingora Backend Router uses consistent hashing to route cacheable requests, with
dozens of separate rings because every combination of compliance and caching features needs
its own. Two changes. First, the `Point` struct was `{hash: u32, index: u32}` = 8 bytes;
the index only needs 16 bits, but Rust alignment keeps the struct at 8 anyway, so they
stored it as `[u8; 6]` with getters — 25% off, and it compiles to the same thing
`repr(packed)` would without the footguns. Second, and bigger: they derived the exact
coefficient of variation for k hashes per server, `CV_k = √((N−1)/(N·k+1))`, and found each
step down in error costs roughly an order of magnitude more hashes. At their working point
of 100,000 hashes per server, the last 90,000 were buying a 0.7% error reduction — and with
32-bit hashes, collisions actually make error worse past ~10,000 hashes per server in large
data centres. So they cut hash count 90%. The operational part is the part to steal:
changing a hash ring moves cache keys, so they carried both rings in memory and picked
per-request via their migration framework, rolling out by data centre rather than by global
percentage to keep cache churn's blast radius small. The v2 ring is in the `pingora-ketama`
crate behind an unadvertised cargo feature.
[writeup](https://blog.cloudflare.com/saving-100-tb-of-ram-with-math/)

### Wild's author reproduced Mold's unflattering benchmark and explained the gap honestly
Mold's updated linker benchmarks included Wild for the first time and showed it 1.2–1.3x
slower on debug links; Wild's own August benchmarks had shown it faster. David Lattimore
reproduced Mold's numbers on different hardware and found the difference was almost entirely
methodology. Mold's harness deletes the output file between runs and passes `--no-fork`;
Wild's ran on tmpfs with the output file already present and forking enabled. On
clang-release that spread is 0.21s vs 0.11s for Wild while Mold barely moves (0.20s vs
0.19s) — a 1.0x ratio becomes 0.6x. Lattimore says tmpfs was probably a mistake since nobody
builds there, and the real gap is Wild lacking `fallocate` pre-allocation and hugepage
mapping for the output file, both already fixed for the next release. Useful as a linker
datapoint, more useful as a reminder that "does the output file already exist" is a
benchmark variable worth controlling in anything that writes a large file.
[post](https://davidlattimore.github.io/posts/2026/09/18/benchmarking-wild-vs-mold.html)

## AI industry

### A chatbot's guess about a ship's cargo nearly put US boarding parties on a Chinese vessel
CNN reports, from four sources, that an intelligence report circulated this spring during
the war with Iran claimed a Chinese ship in the Middle East was carrying nuclear weapons
components. Planes were in the air and armed personnel were preparing to board before
officials looked closer and found the report had been generated with AI help: a Special
Operations Command Pacific analyst had queried a chatbot about the ship's manifest, the bot
fused open-source material with classified SIGINT and misidentified the cargo, and the
analyst then used AI again to package it into the standard report format. One source called
it "entirely false" and said it "almost started a war." SOCPAC and the Pentagon didn't
respond to CNN. The structural detail matters more than the anecdote: adoption is
decentralised, with different commands using different tools under different safety
standards and no single verification standard, and a source says this hallucination wasn't
isolated. The quote to remember is "AI allows you to get to a bad idea faster."
[report](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/18/politics/us-military-ai-false-intelligence-china-ship)

### ZCode uploads your whole repo, and neither toggle in the UI stops it
Ferstar went looking for 700MB of disk in `~/.zcode` and found a 313MB encrypted archive in
`v2/checkpoints/` with a retry counter at 564 failed uploads. Reversing the app's
`app.asar`: the client requests OSS credentials plus a per-round RSA public key from
`zcode.z.ai`, tar.gz's the workspace, encrypts with AES-256-CTR, wraps the key with
RSA-OAEP-SHA256, and POSTs straight to Aliyun OSS. The private key never touches your
machine, so you cannot decrypt the ciphertext sitting on your own disk — and neither can the
client. In his 42,411-file snapshot, `.git` was 86.6% of the payload: LFS cache, the full
object store, and reflogs, meaning credentials deleted in later commits, unpushed branch
names and internal GitLab hostnames all go up. Both plausible-looking settings are decoys:
`optimizeAgentExperienceEnabled` only governs training consent and
`repoSnapshotIndexingEnabled` only governs server-side indexing — the capture sidecar is
instantiated unconditionally at startup with no gating on either, triggering before every
prompt (up to 62 capture events in one session). Deleting the archive just makes a new one
within half an hour. His fix is to empty the directory and set the immutable bit
(`chattr +i` on Linux, `chflags uchg` on macOS); you lose checkpoint rollback, which needed
the upload anyway. Note the blog's own footer says its posts are AI-drafted, but the
evidence chain — manifest, asar strings, socket list — is specific enough to check yourself.
[investigation](https://blog.ferstar.org/en/posts/zcode-silent-workspace-snapshot-upload/)

## AI research + releases

### Your coding agent's harness matters less than you think, except when context is tight
176 matched settings across four models on SWE-Bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2.1, with
the execution loop held fixed while planning, action space and context management vary
independently — which is the useful part, since harnesses are normally compared as whole
systems. Findings worth acting on: context management earns its keep mainly by preventing
context-overflow failures, so its value rises as the window budget tightens and falls when
you have room. Rule-based elision staged before LLM summarisation was the most efficient
strategy, and making elided content recoverable added machinery models barely used for no
accuracy gain. Planning is an accuracy scaffold for weaker models but for stronger ones just
saves cost without moving accuracy. And predefined tools only help models with weak bash
proficiency — bash-capable models do fine with a bash-only interface at substantially lower
cost, especially on command-line tasks. Trajectory analysis backs each one: context
management lengthens trajectories without changing behaviour, planning changes where they
stop, action space changes the granularity of edits.
[paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20804)

## Homelab

### A 27B multimodal model in 5.9GB that keeps 98.2% of its full-precision score
PrismML's Ternary Bonsai 2 27B, released 17 September, compresses Qwen3.8 27B to ternary
{−1, 0, +1} weights with FP16 group-wise scaling — 1.76 effective bits per weight, 5.9GB
total, 262K context, text-and-image input, Apache 2.0. Aggregate benchmark score 83.9
against 85.4 full-precision, which is 98.2% retention and above the Qwen3.6 27B baseline of
83.6. The retention is uneven and the breakdown is the honest part: instruction following
actually goes up (82.66 vs 81.25) and maths barely moves (96.57 vs 97.06), but knowledge and
reasoning drops 2.7 points, vision drops 3.1, and agentic tool calling drops 2.2 to 77.57 —
so the gap is largest in exactly the multi-step workloads where errors compound. Claimed
throughput is up to 143 tok/s on an RTX 5090 and 46.8 tok/s on an M5 Max, with 0.714
mWh/token on a 4090. These are the vendor's own numbers on their own suite; the GGUF and
2-bit MLX builds are on Hugging Face if you'd rather measure it on your own box.
[release](https://prismml.com/news/bonsai-2-27b)
